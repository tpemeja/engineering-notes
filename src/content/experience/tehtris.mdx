## From 3-4 days to 2 hours: provisioning a customer platform

**Problem.** Each new customer got their own instance of the platform, made of several servers on OVH Cloud. Deploying one took 3 to 4 days: servers were set up by hand, each step waited on the previous one, and the deployment team went back and forth with other teams for license provisioning, network configuration and checking the result.

**Constraint.** The product ran on full virtual machines, not containers, and changing that was out of our hands. The solution had to be built around machine images rather than a container platform, which is why we used Packer for images and Airflow to orchestrate the steps.

**Design.** With an engineer in France, I built a self-service pipeline for the deployment team. They enter the customer's details, such as the license key, in a small web app. Packer builds custom OS images with that configuration baked in, and Airflow runs the rest: creating the servers, configuring the network, applying the license, and running setup tests before reporting back. I focused on the build side, the images and the orchestration; my teammate built the intake and reporting app. We split the work by responsibility and unblocked each other across the 8-hour time difference.

**Result.** Deploying a new customer went from 3-4 days to about 2 hours. Other teams still provide the inputs up front, but once they're in, the deployment runs end to end without waiting on anyone.

**What I'd change.** With hundreds of servers to keep running, I'd move to containers, manage the infrastructure as code with Terraform, and possibly run it on Kubernetes. That would have needed more people to set up than our team had at the time, which is why we optimized within the VM model first.

## Opening the Tokyo office: incident triage for APAC

**Context.** In Tokyo, our team of three was the company's first line during APAC hours, outside the working hours of engineering in France and Canada. When something broke, I usually made the call: fix it ourselves, escalate to engineering, or call the CTO directly.

**One incident.** A backend change shipped by R&D broke some scheduled API calls that ran about every minute, and each call now wrote its own log file. The files were tiny, so disk space looked fine, but there were so many that the server ran out of inodes and the disk switched to read-only. Inode usage wasn't monitored, so nothing had warned us.

**Decision.** Before escalating, I traced the problem to its origin: the read-only disk, the inode exhaustion, the flood of small log files, and the API change behind them. Fixing it needed permissions our team didn't have, so I called the CTO directly with the diagnosis in hand, and we fixed it together.

**Follow-up.** I added inode usage to our internal monitoring, so the same failure is detected early next time.
