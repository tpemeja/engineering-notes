## Overview

<VideoEmbed
  youtubeId="wg3SdraT2Oo"
  title="Kataribe demo video"
  caption="The 3:48 demo: two Japanese conversations with English subtitles, the family page Kataribe wrote from them, and my own turn on the live site in English. The grandmother is made up and every voice is AI."

A voice AI that talks with an elderly person in Japanese for a few minutes a
day, and turns what they remember into a life story their family can read and
hear. A *kataribe* (語り部) is a traditional keeper of oral history, the person
who remembers the stories and passes them on.

The voice is only the channel; the product is the loop. Each conversation
becomes stories, people and places on a life timeline, the gaps in that
timeline plan the next conversation, and relatives can send questions the
interviewer asks next time. Nothing reaches the family until the person says
it can.

I built it in four weeks for Google Cloud's AI Builder Cup 2026, and wrote all
of the code myself; my wife helped with the Japanese and with advice along the
way. It is submitted, with a [live site](https://kataribe.kaseovo.com) where
judges try it on their own life, the demo video above and a pitch deck, and
now waits on the judges.

## Problem

- **Remembering takes time.** Someone reaching for a sixty-year-old memory
  pauses for seconds. The Live API ends a turn after 800 ms of silence by
  default, so the interviewer would talk over the memory it asked for.
- **A memoir cannot hold a plausible guess.** The model hears *Shigeru* and
  writes 佐藤繁 when the man is 佐藤茂. Same sound, wrong grandfather, in the
  one document a family keeps.
- **Sharing is the senior's call.** Nothing reaches a relative without her
  agreement, and a relative must not be able to tell what she declined.
- **The bugs that matter only show up live.** Configs that passed every offline
  check died on a live socket, and the 5-10 minute sessions the product needs
  are longer than any quick test.
- **Judges arrive cold, in English.** The product is Japanese and built for one
  senior over many sessions. A judge speaks English, talks for two minutes,
  and must never hear another judge's childhood.

## Approach

Built as vertical slices: each one ends with something that runs and can be
shown, never a half-wired layer waiting on the next.

One rule runs through the code: **the AI never speaks for the person.** A
quote is stored only if it is found word for word in the transcript and heard
in the recording, consent counts only as her own words of agreement, and facts
we already know (her name, birthplace and family) are handed to the model
rather than transcribed.

Behaviour is measured against the live API over several runs and reported as
*n of n*, because a single good run proves little. French and English exist
so the whole loop can be exercised without a Japanese speaker in the room, and
English is also what judges use, but only a Japanese session decides whether
the product works.

<Screenshot
  src="/images/projects/kataribe/console.webp"
  alt="Interview tuning console: a senior picker, a voice picker set to Sulafat, a pause-before-replying slider at 5000 ms with measured guidance, end-of-speech sensitivity set to LOW, an input file picker for replaying a session, and a Start conversation button"
  caption="The tuning console. Pause length and voice are chosen here, validated by the backend and locked into the session's token."
/>

The backend mints a single-use ephemeral token with the whole session config
locked in: prompt, voice, pause length and transcription. The browser streams
16 kHz microphone audio straight to Gemini with it and cannot rewrite any of
those fields. Each session is saved as a stereo WAV (senior left, interviewer
right), and a saved one can be replayed in place of the microphone to compare
two prompts on the same words.

How long the interviewer waits through silence, measured against synthesized
speech:

| Patience | Pause | Result |
|---|---|---|
| 3500 ms | 1.5 s | waited |
| 3500 ms | 2.5 s | **cut in** |
| 6000 ms | 2.5 s | waited |
| 6000 ms | 4.0 s | waited |

The gap the model sees is longer than the pause itself, so the default is
5000 ms.

<Callout variant="warning" title="Affective dialog breaks token sessions">
`enable_affective_dialog` works on a direct API-key connection, but any session
opened through an ephemeral token rejects realtime audio with close code 1007
once it is set, whether locked into the token or asked for by the client.
Emotional sensitivity lives in the interviewer prompt instead.
</Callout>

</Phase>

<Phase
  id="live-evals"
  title="Testing against the live API"
  status="done"
  goal="Test what only appears on a live socket: transcription of names, waiting through a pause, staying in the right language, and sessions as long as the product needs."
  outcome="A live suite streams real audio at the API; the release run passed 15 of 15 browser tests and a six-minute session."
>

Two layers, split by cost:

- **`make check`**, on every push: lint, types, config shape, endpoint
  validation and the resampler maths. Offline and fast.
- **`make eval`**, by hand before a release and after any change to how the
  interviewer speaks or listens: synthesized Japanese streamed at the real API
  in real time, through the same token path the browser uses. It checks the
  transcript, the patience setting and the reply's rules, graded partly by
  code and partly by a model judging against the prompt itself.

**`make spar`** goes further and puts the interviewer in a live conversation
with an AI playing the person being interviewed. Both sides are real audio,
so pauses and turn-taking are genuine. Each persona presses one rule:

| Persona | Behaviour | The interviewer must |
|---|---|---|
| `haru` | Pauses two or three seconds mid-memory | not fill the silence |
| `shigeru` | Refuses to discuss the war | drop it |
| `kimiko` | Drifts to another subject | follow rather than steer back |

The release run, before submitting:

| Suite | Result |
|---|---|
| Browser tests, a judge's whole path included | 15 of 15 |
| Six-minute session in a real browser | no drop |
| Live API checks | 38 of 40, both failures traced and fixed, then 6 of 6 |

<Callout variant="lesson" title="The server was never hanging up">
Sparring sessions kept dying at 70-130 seconds with `1011 keepalive ping
timeout`. It looked like a harness quirk, but it killed the interviewer's own
test session too, and no test had run long enough to notice. The SDK hands
Python's websockets library its default 20 second ping timeout, and the Live
API does not reliably pong inside it, so the client itself hung up on a
healthy connection. With the timeout off, one connection ran 391 seconds. The
browser was never affected, and the six-minute browser run keeps it that way.
</Callout>

</Phase>

<Phase
  id="conversation-feel"
  title="Sounding like a conversation"
  status="done"
  goal="Have the interviewer open the conversation itself, and stop answering every turn with the same acknowledgement followed by a question."
  outcome="The interviewer greets first, and 3 of 6 sparring turns now react without asking anything."
>

A sparring run showed exactly what a tester had reported: stiff and
relentlessly interrogative. The prompt said to keep acknowledgements short,
but never said a turn could be *only* an acknowledgement, so it always asked.

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| そうでしたか。…もう少しお聞かせいただけますか。 | それは立派な柿ですね。 |
| そうですか。…どんなお料理にされるのですか。 | まあ、それは愛らしいですね。 |
| Same acknowledgement, then a question, every turn | 6 turns, 3 with no question, acknowledgements varied |

Three rules went into the prompt in all three languages: a turn may end
without a question, an acknowledgement is never reused, and a reaction says
what the story did to you rather than repeating it. When a session opens, a
short stage direction ("they have just sat down, speak to them gently") makes
the interviewer greet first, so nobody has to work out that they should start.

<Callout variant="warning" title="Client content leaves a Live session deaf">
Sending the stage direction with `sendClientContent` produces a good greeting,
then the session stops answering the audio that follows, and sometimes closes
with 1006. `sendRealtimeInput({ text })` greets just as well and the
conversation carries on.
</Callout>

</Phase>

<Phase

The model is asked for the person's own words, and every quote then passes
three checks before it is stored:

1. **Word for word in the transcript**, ignoring only spacing and punctuation.
2. **Written in her language's script.** A Korean particle inside a Japanese
   quote once passed the first check, because the transcript had it too.
3. **Heard in the recording.** Gemini Flash listens to the clip the family
   would hear and confirms the words. It caught the Live API writing 私は as
   私を, unprompted, in a browser test.

A quote that fails any of them is dropped rather than kept: a memoir is the
one place a plausible paraphrase must not end up. Stories land on six life
stages, from childhood to later life, and the next session opens with a word
about the last one.

<Callout variant="decision" title="No prompt can fix a homophone">
佐藤茂 and 佐藤繁 both read *Shigeru*, so asking the model to be careful
changes nothing, and listening to the recording cannot tell them apart either.
The fix is to stop transcribing what we already know: the senior's name, its
reading, birth year, birthplace and family go into the prompt from their
profile. Names outside the profile (the first boss, the neighbour) still have
the problem.
</Callout>

</Phase>

<Phase
  id="consent"
  title="Consent before sharing"
  status="done"
  goal="Let a story reach the family only when the person agreed to share it, and keep every other story off the family page entirely."
  outcome="A story is shared only on her own words of agreement, found in the transcript: 36 of 36 runs."
>

The interviewer asks once, at the close: it names today's stories in a few
words and asks whether the family may read them, and whether one should stay
hers. Extraction returns the words she used to agree, and `verify` checks them
against the transcript and the recording exactly as it checks quotes. No
words, or words that are not there, and the story stays private.

Twelve cases, measured against the real model, three runs each:

| What she said | Result |
|---|---|
| A clear yes | shared |
| A no, or a hesitation: 「うーん、そうですねえ……どうしましょうかねえ」 | private, never asked again |
| Yes to one story, no to another, in one sentence | each one as she said |
| 「あの話は家族に見せないで」, about a story from last time | taken back |
| No reply, or never asked | private, asked again at the next close |
| **All twelve cases** | **36 of 36** |

Private stories are filtered out in the query, so there is no route by which
one arrives and is merely not rendered. A browser test checks that not a word
of one reaches the page, and the page does not even show a count of them.

The opening of the next session, with the family question in it:

> こんにちは、田中ハルさん。前回伺ったお父様の織物の工場のお話を、今もよく覚えています。今日はお孫さんのゆかさんが、結婚式のときのお着物のことを知りたがっていらっしゃいましたよ。どんなお着物でしたか?

The rules around it:

- **Who is asking is the point.** The interviewer always says who the question
  is from, so the form asks for the relation as well as the name (孫のゆか).
- **At most two per session, oldest first.** It is her story, not a queue.
- **Nothing she refused is asked, even by family.** Each question is screened
  against the avoid list before it is stored, and again whenever she refuses
  something new. A held question looks exactly like one still waiting.
- **The answer is linked, not copied.** If she kept it private, the question
  still reads as waiting, the same as if she declined.

Against the real models: screening 3 of 3, reporting which questions were
answered 9 of 9.

<Callout variant="lesson" title="'When the conversation allows' means never">
The first prompt said to ask the family question when the conversation
allowed. Given a choice of topic, the interviewer always picked something
else. Making it the opening topic fixed that, but pushed the recall of last
time out of the opening in 3 of 3 runs. The opening now has an order (last
time first, then the family question), and it has held 6 of 6.
</Callout>

</Phase>

What the start page does for a stranger:

- **A persona of their own.** Memory is per person, so with one shared persona
  the second judge would be greeted with the first judge's childhood. *Try it*
  asks for a name and makes a visitor whose link is 128 random bits. Talk
  again, and it remembers.
- **A path in the corner.** Watch the demo, read its family page, see a
  family question, talk, read your own page. Each step ticks itself off, and
  *Go* takes you there from any page.
- **A close that fits two minutes.** At two minutes the interviewer asks
  whether to go on. When they stop, it asks one thing, alone in its turn: may
  the family read today's story. *Keep it private* on screen beats a spoken
  yes; *Share* never beats a spoken no.
- **A read-only example.** The demo's family page shows the question form, but
  nothing is sent from it, so judges never read each other's questions.
- **A cap.** 30 visitor conversations a day at about $0.10 each, so the worst
  case over the whole evaluation stays under $60.

<Callout variant="lesson" title="The sharing question got lost in the goodbye">
Trying it as a judge, the interviewer asked a question, thanked me, asked
whether the family could read the story and said goodbye, all in one turn.
My reply was "So do you want to... make it private or not?", and the buttons
to choose only appeared on the end screen. Now the question gets a turn of its
own, and the buttons show from that moment.
</Callout>

## Future improvements

- **Native Japanese review.** The demo was voiced by AI and judges talk in
  English, so prompt changes have gone unreviewed in the language that ships.
  Whether a light restatement like 〜なんですね reads as warm aizuchi or as
  parroting is a native speaker's call.
- **Names heard only aloud.** A name outside the profile can still be
  misheard, and once even the person's own name, after they introduced
  themselves. The interviewer should hold to the profile's name over what it
  hears, and a relative could fix a spelling in one tap.
- **Exact audio for each quote.** The Live API accepts `word_timestamp` and
  returns no word offsets, so a quote's clip runs from one interviewer turn to
  the next. A transcription pass over the stored recording would make it
  exact.
- **Presence detection.** The tablet noticing someone sitting nearby and
  inviting them, instead of a button.
- **Sharing beyond the family.** The start page already marks it *Next*: the
  teller choosing a friend, a care worker or a local archive as readers.
- **Notifications.** Relatives have to come back to see whether an answer
  landed.
